# A Story of Machine Learning in Big Companies

[English](README.md) | [中文](README.zh.md)

## Preface
Let's face it, machine learning has always been a bit of a beast, a multi-headed hydra of knowledge. But here's the kicker, folks: many a data scientist, after painstakingly training a model, has had that "oh, sugar honey iced tea" moment. They realize that wrestling the model into submission during training was just the warm-up act. The real boss battles? Deploying that sucker, keeping it retrained, and — gasp! — monitoring it in the wild. Suddenly, those quiet nights are filled with the silent screams of data scientists muttering, "I'm not DevOps! I don't want to be DevOps! Can't I just be a peace-loving Data Scientist?!"

And yet, bless their hearts, their woes often pale in comparison to the other big headache: the bigwigs upstairs who, bless their little cotton socks, don't quite get machine learning or, for that matter, the whole "model pipeline" jazz. These seasoned veterans, often scarred by past projects that went sideways faster than a greased weasel, have learned the hard way that "crawl, walk, run" is the only way to avoid a full-blown corporate catastrophe. "Big bang" approaches? More like "big boom." But here's the rub: big companies have big processes, big security fences, big rulebooks, and, of course, big departmental silos. And besides the technobabble, what keeps these managers up at night?

"How many teams do we need, and what on earth will they do?"
"Data scientists keep screaming for 'production data.' Are they mad? How is that even possible?"
"I thought one team of data scientists could handle all this! Now they need DevOps? I thought they looked down on those DevOps folks?!"
Alright, buckle up, buttercups, because here's my two cents.

The Wisdom of an Old Aquarian
As an old Aquarian who's bounced around more roles than a rubber ball in a hurricane, I've got a pretty good handle on the quirks, limitations, and pains of pretty much every corporate gig. Big companies are rarely short on smart cookies; you want to be a one-person army? Fine. You just want to stick to your lane? Also fine. And they're never in a rush, either. Their secret sauce for survival? "Let the little guys make all the mistakes, then we'll just poach their talent once they figure it out." It's a classic.

What big companies really fear is spinning their wheels. And it's not because they lack direction; it's because they have too many directions. It's not that there's nobody to do the work; it's that there are too many people trying to do the work. Small companies whine about being understaffed, joking about "one person changing 10 lightbulbs at once." Me? I think "10 people trying to change one lightbulb" in a big company is way more agonizing! In the grand corporate scheme, I've found the secret to success boils down to three words: Consensus, Consensus, and more Consensus.

This codebase? It's a collection of 24 stories (with more on the way!), representing 24 months – two years. The first year is dubbed "Growth," and the second, "Change." (If we keep going, they'll be "Progress" and "Flourishing" – catchy, right?). Together, these stories map out the ideal journey of a machine learning project in a big company. (Why "ideal"? Because it assumes we hit a milestone every single month. In a "10 people, one lightbulb" scenario, just debating whose hand is allowed to touch the bulb could easily eat up a month!)

These tales are primarily for the management crowd, aiming to give them a quick and dirty guide to the "crawl, walk, run" approach for ML projects and address those nagging questions keeping them up at night. You'll find all the stories in the "story" branch, available in both Chinese and English.

And to prove I'm not just all talk and no trousers, each story has a corresponding code branch. For example, "G_Jan" covers what we'd get done in January, aligning perfectly with the story.

We all know our main gig, but let's be real, we all have a side hustle – a "bonus skill" or two. A data scientist might dabble in DevOps; a manager might actually understand a bit of tech. My hope is that everyone, from the core players to those with sweet side skills, finds something valuable here. Let these stories spark some good old-fashioned water cooler chatter.

And then, one day, it hits you: "Aha! We've finally reached consensus!"
